Inverse ocean displacement

A choppy ocean spectrum moves points horizontally as well as vertically. Projecting collision geometry onto the deformed water and subtracting a sampled offset is not the inverse of the ocean mapping. Use the displacement field and its Jacobian to solve for the source coordinates whose forward deformation reaches the desired position.

Surface derivatives describe how the normal and tangent directions change. The fundamental forms and shape operator give the principal curvatures and directions on an evaluated OpenSubdiv limit surface, rather than relying only on the faceted control mesh.

Shape matching and point-cloud registration

With corresponding points, covariance and SVD give a least-squares rigid fit. When correspondence is unknown, iterative closest point alternates matching and transform estimation. Both are useful foundations for registration and geometry tools, with initialization and outliers still important.

PCA · SVD · Rigid fitting · ICP

Velocity gradients and field decomposition

A velocity-gradient tensor reveals local stretching, compression and rotation. Helmholtz decomposition tackles a different problem: splitting a field into curl-free and divergence-free contributions, with domain and boundary terms accounted for. These methods let you inspect and alter specific parts of field behavior.

Velocity gradients · Strain · Helmholtz

Adaptive subdivision for facial fracture

Extreme facial-fracture close-ups in X-Men: Dark Phoenix needed very high levels of localized refinement. OpenSubdiv could not stitch the separately refined regions as required, and Houdini’s other subdivision approach became prohibitively slow. A custom Catmull-Clark implementation in VEX delivered adaptive refinement with cook times measured in seconds on the production setup.

Adaptive subdivision · Catmull-Clark · Performance

Surface gradients and traced effects

The facial fracture needed a direction for each crack to open. Starting from a geodesic-distance attribute, compute its gradient directly on the mesh instead of converting the geometry to a volume. The gradient also gives directions orthogonal to contour lines and supports surface tracing, including lightning crawling over Storm in X-Men: Apocalypse. For heightfields, sample the volume directly without an intermediate polygon mesh.

Geodesics · Attribute gradients · Isolines · Streamlines

Volume 1 · Point clouds

Point-cloud filtering and occlusion

Modern point searches such as pcfind() do not provide the same smooth filtering built into pcopen(). Implement that filtering when the task needs it, rather than dismissing the older functions. Camera occlusion with variable pscale presents another gap: larger points can hide later points, so the selection order and each point’s radius have to be handled explicitly.

pcopen · pcfind · Smooth filtering · Variable pscale

Point-based caustics with analytic intersections

Start with the caustic pattern you need. Refract sampled rays through a deformed water surface, intersect a ground plane analytically where that model applies, and accumulate the hits. Rasterizing the points into a volume removes sparse dot patterns without requiring huge point counts. Sharpen the field with an unsharp-mask kernel when the result needs it.

Refraction · Analytic intersections · Rasterization · Kernels

Implementation decisions

What matters beyond the algorithm

Function behavior, memory use, topology, accuracy and the visual result all affect the solution. The lessons examine those decisions on actual geometry.

Know the operation

Look past the function name

Understand why nearpoints() delegates to pcfind(), and why pcopen() still matters when smooth point-cloud filtering is required.

Use the right representation

Avoid unnecessary conversions

Compute gradients on geometry, sample heightfields in their native volume form, and use analytic intersections when the geometry permits.

Build for production

Profile and preserve data

Use optimized traversal, hashing, multithreaded construction, attribute-preserving fusing and reusable HDAs where the task calls for them.

Methods are developed and tested before recording. Code, diagrams and intermediate results remain visible; repetitive typing, cooking and navigation are shortened in the edit.

Before choosing a course

Common questions

Can I start with Volume 2?

Yes. Volume 1 and Volume 2 are standalone advanced courses. Each develops its own topics from the prerequisites needed inside that course. Choose the volume whose methods are most useful to you, or bundle both.

Is Volume 1 outdated because it began in an earlier Houdini version?

No. Volume 1 has been updated for Houdini 21. The version label describes the current maintained course content, not the age of the first recording.

What knowledge is assumed?

Basic Houdini and VEX familiarity is assumed. The courses do not reteach introductory syntax or interface basics. Supporting documentation is referenced when a prerequisite API or concept is needed.

Do I need advanced mathematics?

No advanced mathematics background is required. The lessons start with a practical Houdini problem. When mathematics helps solve it, the relevant ideas are explained visually and implemented in VEX. You also learn how to choose algorithms, test performance and accuracy, and work around the limits of existing tools.

Are scene files and supporting material included?

Yes. The individual courses and the bundle include scene files and supporting course material associated with the released lessons.
