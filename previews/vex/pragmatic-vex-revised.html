Why Pragmatic VEX

Stop reading SIGGRAPH papers like a foreign language.

Nothing here is taught for its own sake. Each topic starts from a production problem, finds the mathematics that solves it, and ends as working VEX. Along the way the decompositions, tensors and differential operators become what dot and cross products already are to you: tools you think with.

Problem first.

Nobody needs a shape operator. You need the curvature of a heightfield, and the shape operator is how you get it. Hashing appears because points have to be matched back to their originals after subdivision, not because hashing is a topic. Everything is introduced that way round.

Everything works on a box.

The examples are production geometry: a head mesh that gets adaptive cracks, not a grid or a Möbius strip. A character mesh shows the problems a box never will, and brute force does not get you past them. You also end up with something worth looking at.

When the stock tool is not enough.

Too slow, not accurate enough, or missing the one thing the shot needs. You build the replacement and see where it wins, like an adaptive subdivision that cooks in seconds at levels where the stock node is no longer usable.

Know what Houdini is really doing.

nearpoints() is not the faster lookup many people take it for; it is pcfind() under another name. pgfind() is explained from how it works inside. Lessons test assumptions, profile the code, and show the practices that hold up in production.

What it lets you do

Replace the workaround with the computation.

Eight examples out of several hundred topics. Each one started as a production problem.

Image placeholderA velocity field beside its divergence-free and curl-free parts

Volume 2 · Velocity fields

Take a velocity field apart. Control what you put back.

A flow can look right and still carry expansion you did not ask for, too much spin, or stretching in the wrong direction. The velocity-gradient tensor shows which of those is happening, and where. Helmholtz decomposition then splits the field into its divergence-free and curl-free parts, so you can change one and rebuild the field instead of scaling all of it.

Image placeholderCollision geometry in displaced ocean space and in rest space

Volume 2 · Oceans IN DEVELOPMENT

Invert the ocean. Stop raycasting it.

An ocean spectrum moves water sideways as well as up, so the water under a hull did not start directly beneath it. The usual fix is to ray the collision geometry onto the displaced surface and subtract the displacement found there, an approximation that gets worse as the chop increases. The displacement is a mapping with a Jacobian. Invert it, and the collision geometry is carried into the undisplaced ocean exactly.

Image placeholderPrincipal curvature directions drawn as a cross field on a character

Volume 2 · Curvature

Curvature with a direction.

Say you need the curvature of a heightfield or an SDF, and the direction it runs in. That is what the shape operator gives you: two principal curvatures and their directions. On an OpenSubdiv limit surface they are evaluated analytically, so they hold still when the mesh resolution changes. Run cracks along ridges, wrinkles across folds, wear over convex edges only.

Image placeholderA point cloud converging onto its target over ICP iterations

Volume 2 · Fitting

Recover a transform from nothing but points.

Two point clouds of the same object and no transform between them: a cached simulation, a scan, a deforming piece you need to lock something to. Build the covariance matrix, take its SVD, and the best-fit rotation and translation come straight out. Add iterative closest point and it still works when you do not know which point matches which.

Image placeholderAdaptive subdivision on a face in close-up, refined only where the fracture is

Volume 1 · Subdivision

Adaptive subdivision that survives a close-up.

The extreme close-ups in X-Men: Dark Phoenix needed sub-pixel detail, which means adaptive subdivision at very high levels. OpenSubdiv does not stitch the borders between subdivided groups, and the cook time of Houdini’s own algorithm grows quadratically, which rules it out long before level 12. Catmull-Clark written in VEX, with adaptivity and boundary rules built in, cooks in seconds.

Image placeholderGeodesic distance on a head, with its gradient drawn as vectors

Volume 1 · Gradients

Give a crack a direction to open in.

The face cracks in X-Men: Dark Phoenix had to know which way to open. Geodesic distance is only a number per point; its gradient is the direction. Houdini has no gradient for attributes, so it is computed directly on the mesh. The usual detour through a volume and back ties the result to the voxel size and is less exact.

Image placeholderPool caustics: raw points beside the rasterised, sharpened result

Volume 1 · Caustics

Caustics without the dots.

Points are refracted through a deformed water surface and carried to the ground analytically, by projection onto a plane instead of intersection tests against polygons: faster, lighter and more accurate. Rasterising them into a volume removes the dotty look without needing far more points, and an unsharp mask written in VEX sharpens the pattern.

Image placeholderPoints of varying pscale seen from camera, before and after occlusion culling

Volume 1 · Point clouds

Cull what the camera cannot see, by radius.

Culling hidden points is a simple optimisation until the points have different sizes: a large point in front should hide the small ones behind it, and the stock point cloud functions cannot express that. The same chapter rebuilds the smooth filtering that pcopen() has and pcfind() does not, so you are not tied to the old functions to get it.

Gradient Descent · 3D

Trace anything over a surface.

Follow a gradient across a mesh and you have the basis for water drops running down geometry, directions flowing over a character, or lightning crawling over a body, as built for Storm in X-Men: Apocalypse.

Gradient Descent · Planar

From a scalar to a direction.

Start with a value on the geometry, such as geodesic distance, and ask how to turn it into a direction. That gradient is computed on the geometry itself. Contour lines come from the same result, because they run at right angles to it.

Gradient Descent · Heightfields

Heightfields, without converting them.

Converting a heightfield to polygons is slow, heavy on memory and tied to the resolution. Here the same tracing samples the heightfield volume directly.
