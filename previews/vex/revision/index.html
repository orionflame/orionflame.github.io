Why Pragmatic VEX

Learn the method behind the result.

The goal is not to collect unfamiliar terminology or copy finished setups. Each subject connects a production problem to the geometric or field quantities that describe it, then follows the reasoning into a VEX implementation you can inspect, modify and reuse.

01

Start with the problem.

Identify what needs to move, align, spread, deform, smooth, separate or remain constrained. The mathematics enters as a language for describing that behavior precisely, not as the product itself.

02

Make the quantities visible.

See how gradients, eigenvectors, curvature, strain, divergence and other quantities appear on real geometry and fields. Inspect the intermediate results before they become part of a larger tool.

03

Build a reusable solution.

Follow the coordinate spaces, numerical choices, boundary conditions and implementation details that affect the outcome. Then adapt the same reasoning to a different shot, asset or procedural system.

Techniques that travel

One idea can solve many production problems.

The value of a method is not limited to the example used to teach it. Once the underlying quantity is understood, the same reasoning can be redirected toward very different effects and tools.

Production example

Gradients and geodesics

Use surface-aware direction and distance to drive flow fields, crack propagation, traveling effects, lightning paths, growth and placement that follows a character or asset rather than world space. Related gradient and geodesic methods were used in the adaptive facial fracture system developed for X-Men: Dark Phoenix.

Analysis & fitting

PCA, SVD, least squares and ICP

Estimate orientation, stabilize local frames, fit transforms, register samples, compute principal axes and build shape-matching tools that remain useful far beyond the original example.

Surface behavior

Curvature and principal directions

Find ridges, valleys and directional surface structure for masks, adaptive detail, meshing, wear, smoothing, relaxation and art-directed effects that need to respond to shape.

Field control

Velocity gradients and Helmholtz decomposition

Separate expansion, rotation, deformation, potential-driven motion and divergence-free motion so you can edit the cause you care about instead of scaling an entire field and hoping for a better result.

Geometry algorithms

Custom subdivision and limit surfaces

Extend Catmull-Clark rules beyond stock controls, evaluate smooth surfaces directly and build adaptive refinement or sampling tools around the needs of a specific asset or effect.

A worked example from Volume 2

Take a velocity field apart.
Control what you put back.

A velocity field can look plausible while hiding unwanted expansion, excessive rotation or deformation in the wrong directions. Analyze its velocity-gradient tensor to expose stretching, compression and spin, then use Helmholtz decomposition to separate divergence-free and curl-free components.

Instead of scaling the entire field, isolate a contribution, modify it and reconstruct the result under the constraints your tool requires.

The Eigenveil of Entropy

1. Matrix foundations and decompositions
  • Interpret matrices as transformations, projections and changes of basis. Vector spaces, tensors, transposition, diagonal, orthogonal and orthonormal matrices.
  • Use eigenvalue decomposition, singular value decomposition, principal component analysis and polar decomposition to expose the structure of geometric data.
  • Work with pseudoinverses and matrix norms when a system is rectangular, rank-deficient or needs a measurable error. Moore-Penrose inverse, 1-norm, infinity norm, max norm, Frobenius norm and spectral norm.
2. Least squares, fitting and registration
  • Fit lines, planes and multivariate data with linear least squares, regression and covariance analysis.
  • Estimate normals, principal axes and rigid transforms from point clouds using outer products, covariance matrices, PCA and SVD.
  • Build shape matching and point-cloud registration methods. Rigid transform matching, ICP, affine transformations, homogeneous coordinates and coordinate systems.
3. Bounding volumes and convex geometry
  • Construct oriented bounding boxes and cylinders for geometric analysis, temporal bounds and collision-query workflows.
  • Compute minimum-area and minimum-volume bounds using 2D and 3D convex hulls and rotating-calipers reasoning. MAR, OBB, OBC, MVB, MVC, temporal OBB and temporal OBC.
4. Differential geometry and surface analysis
  • Build first and second surface derivatives, fundamental forms and the shape operator (Weingarten map).
  • Compute principal curvatures and directions, mean and Gaussian curvature, normal curvature, curvedness, shape index and curvature tensors.
  • Analyze ridges, valleys, asymptotic directions, umbilics, extrema and local surface type. Synclastic, anticlastic and monoclastic regions; Willmore energy; minimum and maximum principal curvature.
  • Apply the same reasoning to heightfields, terrain and geomorphometric analysis.
5. OpenSubdiv and intrinsic surface methods
  • Evaluate analytic OpenSubdiv derivatives, normals, curvature and intrinsic UV coordinates on limit surfaces.
  • Measure and compare intrinsic distances. Geodesic, diffusion and biharmonic distance.
  • Use differential and intrinsic information for surface smoothing, relaxation and shape analysis.
6. Differential operators and numerical methods
  • Compute gradient, divergence, curl, Jacobian, Hessian and Laplacian on scalar and vector fields.
  • Build finite-difference and discrete Laplacian stencils on volumes and lattices. FDM, VDB analysis, Gaussian derivatives and volume discretization.
  • Use root finding, numerical integration and quadratic methods as supporting tools inside larger algorithms.
7. Flow, deformation and continuum analysis
  • Decompose velocity gradients into symmetric and antisymmetric parts to expose strain, strain rate, spin, vorticity, expansion and volume change.
  • Analyze deformation gradients, principal deformations and their directions in continuum and material-based workflows.
  • Diagnose vortical structures and field behavior. Q-criterion, Lambda2, helicity, stress tensors and Galilean invariance.
  • Connect these quantities to methods used in fluid and MPM-style analysis without treating the surrounding solver as a black box.
8. PDEs, field decomposition and reconstruction
  • Formulate Laplace and biharmonic problems with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions.
  • Separate vector fields into solenoidal, irrotational and harmonic components using Helmholtz decomposition.
  • Reconstruct fields after editing individual contributions and use the same framework for minimal surfaces, fills and constrained smoothing.
9. Sampling and interpolation
  • Implement trilinear, tricubic, cubic and smooth volume sampling.
  • Interpolate matrices, directions and rotations. SLERP, directional attribute filling and closest-point sampling.

1. Point-cloud search, filtering and distribution
  • Build grid-based point-cloud lookup and smooth filtering with pgfind() and related search strategies.
  • Distribute points across polygonal surfaces using relaxation, self-proximity, uniform packing and tighter packing methods.
  • Apply clustering, camera-aware occlusion, variable point scale and geometric advection.
2. Generic kernels, filtering and data structures
  • Generalize VEX kernels so the same processing pattern can be reused across attributes and operators.
  • Implement convolution, attribute blur and sharpening, cubic Hermite interpolation and shaping functions.
  • Use hashing, unique-pair matching, operator decoupling and loop unrolling to support scalable tools.
3. Fields, gradients and curve tracing
  • Compute attribute gradients and use gradient descent on arbitrary polygonal geometry and heightfields.
  • Trace isolines, contour curves, streamlines and flowlines through scalar and vector fields.
  • Build forward and backward tracing, geometry traversal and field-visualization tools.
4. Topology, edges and geometry construction
  • Traverse K-depth neighborhoods through edges and primitives using half-edge structures.
  • Split edges, optimize edge operations and create geometry across multiple threads.
  • Build custom fusing with attribute interpolation, attribute preservation and group preservation.
5. Custom subdivision and limit-surface evaluation
  • Implement custom Catmull-Clark and bilinear subdivision in VEX, then extend the rules beyond stock controls.
  • Handle boundary rules, adaptive refinement and quadtree-driven subdivision.
  • Evaluate OpenSubdiv-compatible limit surfaces, sample smooth positions and frames, and display isolines directly on the limit surface.
  • Build the orthonormal frames and supporting geometric operations required by the implementation.
6. Applied studies
  • Combine field tracing, geometry processing and sampling methods in complete applications.
  • Build sea and pool caustics as examples of turning the underlying ideas into production-oriented procedural tools.
7. Performance and reusable tool design
  • Profile VEX and geometry-processing workloads, identify the real bottleneck and apply targeted optimizations.
  • Design reusable HDAs and operators whose algorithms are separated from their interface and asset-specific assumptions.

Common questions

Can I start with Volume 2?

Yes. Volume 1 and Volume 2 are standalone advanced courses. Each develops its own topics from the prerequisites needed inside that course. Choose the volume whose methods are most useful to you, or bundle both.

Is Volume 1 outdated because it began in an earlier Houdini version?

No. Volume 1 has been updated for Houdini 21. The version label describes the current maintained course content, not the age of the first recording.

What knowledge is assumed?

Basic Houdini and VEX familiarity is assumed. The courses do not reteach introductory syntax or interface basics. Supporting documentation is referenced when a prerequisite API or concept is needed.

How is mathematics taught in Pragmatic VEX?

Mathematical ideas are explained through geometric and visual intuition, then developed into working VEX implementations. The emphasis is on why the method works, which assumptions and numerical choices matter, and how to adapt the same reasoning to other geometry, field, deformation and simulation problems.
